IA market where you can see the value

Most of what is known about how fast prices absorb news comes from stock markets, and a stock market is an awkward laboratory: nobody observes what a share is worth, so the speed and the accuracy of a price’s reaction can only be judged against a model of it. A baseball game is a cleaner experiment. Its news arrives on a schedule, a pitch every twenty seconds or so. Each piece is public, MLB stamps it to the millisecond, and what it is worth can be worked out from the inning, the score, the bases, the outs and the count.

Kalshi, an exchange regulated by the CFTC, lists a market on every MLB game: a contract that pays $1 if a team wins. Its public archive keeps every trade, to the microsecond, with the price, the size and which side crossed the spread to make it. I downloaded all of them for the 2025 and 2026 seasons and lined each one up against MLB’s record of the pitch that came before it.

For every pitch I measure three things. The fair move: how much the pitch should change the price, given where the market stood a second before it. The response: where the market actually traded at each instant over the next two minutes. And what every trade earned, judged one minute later.

IILatency

Nothing happens before the pitch. Five seconds before it, the price has absorbed {{beta_m5}} of the move the pitch is about to cause. Then it moves fast. The price the market trades at is halfway to its new level {{t50}} seconds after the pitch (95% interval {{t50_lo}}–{{t50_hi}}) and 90% of the way there by {{t90}}, before the next pitch, which comes a median {{gap_median}} seconds after the last.

That curve waits for trades on both sides of the market, so it is the patient measure. The impatient one is the first trade that goes more than halfway to the new price. On the {{big26}} big pitches of 2026, the ones that moved fair value by four points or more, it came a median {{half_med}} seconds after the pitch: within one second {{within1}} of the time, within two seconds {{within2}}, within five {{within5}}.

IVWho pays for speed

Someone is on the other side of every fast trade. Kalshi runs an order book: a maker posts a price and waits; a taker crosses the spread and trades against it. If a maker’s quote is still resting when a home run clears the fence, the fastest taker gets it at the old price.

Measured a minute later, makers earned {{maker_c}}¢ per contract across 2026, before Kalshi’s maker fee of about {{maker_fee_c}}¢. Where they earned it, and where they gave it back, depends almost entirely on the second in which the trade happened (Fig. 6). Trades in the five seconds after a pitch that moved fair value by two points or more were {{after_news_share}} of all contracts, and they cost makers {{after_news_c}}¢ a contract. Every other trade earned makers {{rest_c}}¢. Netted over the season, makers handed {{given_back}} of their ordinary trading profit back in those five-second windows (95% interval {{gb_lo}}–{{gb_hi}}): about ${{gb_dollars}} a game, or ${{tax_total}} across 2026, against ${{rest_total}} earned on every other trade.

A resting quote is an option written for free to whoever sees the play first. Most of the time it expires harmlessly; after a pitch that moves the price it gets exercised, and quickly: {{loss_15}} of the makers’ loss in those windows comes within a second and a half of the pitch, {{loss_3}} within three seconds. The orders on the other side, takers trading in the direction of the news within five seconds of such a pitch, were {{snipe_share}} of contracts. They made {{snipe_c}}¢ a contract, against a taker fee of about {{snipe_fee_c}}¢.

VRhythm

The pitch clock gives this market something a stock market sees a few times a year: news on a timetable. Everyone knows when the next piece of news is coming, but nobody knows which pitch will matter, and the tape shows it. Across all pitches, trading barely changes as the pitch approaches: {{pulse_before}} orders a second per game in the last half second against {{pulse_base}} in the middle of the interval. Around a big pitch, it holds at about {{big_before}} a second until the ball is on its way, then doubles, to {{big_peak}} a second {{big_peak_t}} seconds after the pitch, and takes several seconds to settle (Fig. 7).

VIIChecks and limits

A simulated market with known answers. Kalshi’s archive keeps trades, not quotes, so every price here is read off the tape: a taker who buys pays the ask, one who sells hits the bid, and the latest of each brackets the midpoint. To check that this measures what I say it does, I simulate {{sim_games}} games where the truth is known: a fair value that jumps after each pitch, makers who reprice after random delays, noise traders at the real tape’s rates, and fast traders who hit stale quotes. The first trade past halfway always comes after the true repricing, by a median of {{sim_lag}} seconds; the curve read off the trades trails the true quotes by at most {{sim_gap}} of the move and has caught up within seven seconds (Fig. 10); and the makers’ one-minute profits come back within {{sim_markout}} of the truth.

A placebo. Moving each pitch ten seconds early, the price drifts {{placebo}} of the coming move (95% interval {{placebo_lo}} to {{placebo_hi}}). Nothing leaks before the pitch.

No look-ahead. The price before a pitch uses only trades from more than a second before it. Every Kalshi market is matched to its MLB game by teams, date and the moment the market closed, which Kalshi does right after the final out, and every matched market settled the way the final score says it should: {{settle26}} in 2026 and {{settle25}} in 2025.

Limits. Quotes are seen only when someone trades against them, so the times here are upper bounds by a fraction of a second. The clock is MLB’s own pitch timestamp, and whatever delay MLB’s system has is inside every number. The fair move comes from an average-team model shifted to the market’s view, which ignores who is batting and pitching. One-minute markouts include the next few pitches’ news, which averages out but adds noise. Fees follow Kalshi’s published schedule for this series. This is one exchange, and nothing here is a recommendation to trade.

VIIIData and code

Everything on this page is produced by one pipeline: make data downloads the trades (Kalshi’s public trade API), the play-by-play with both pitch timestamps (MLB Stats API) and the pitch-level game states and win expectancy (Baseball Savant); make all builds the tables, runs the event study, the analysis, the simulation and {{n_tests}} tests, and writes this page, filling every number from the outputs. The raw trades stay on disk and are not republished; the code, the method and the aggregated results are on GitHub.

Related work. The idea that a resting quote is a free option for better-informed traders is Copeland and Galai’s (“Information Effects on the Bid-Ask Spread,” Journal of Finance, 1983). Measuring the races to pick off stale quotes from exchange messages is Aquilina, Budish and O’Neill’s (“Quantifying the High-Frequency Trading ‘Arms Race’,” Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2022). The closest study in sports is Croxson and Reade’s, on how betting-exchange prices absorb goals in soccer (“Information and Efficiency: Goal Arrival in Soccer Betting,” Economic Journal, 2014). Baseball adds a pitch-level clock and a fair value for every pitch.
